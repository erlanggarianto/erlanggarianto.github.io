Redesigning an EdTech Mobile App: From ~4K to ~712K Certificates in One Year

Rumah Siap Kerja's app was confusing people at every step. I rebuilt the information architecture, simplified every core flow, and built a component system. A year later, the platform had grown from ~8K to ~574K+ customers.

Overview

The Short Version

Quick backstory. Rumah Siap Kerja (RSK) is an Indonesian edtech platform where people take job-skills video courses, paid with Kartu Prakerja, a government voucher program. Paying means redeeming a code you buy on a marketplace like Tokopedia or Bukalapak, so it is not exactly a standard checkout.

The program was about to scale up fast, and a confusing app would send all that new traffic straight to competitors. My research showed people genuinely wanted to learn, but the app kept getting in their way: unclear payment buttons, a banner-heavy homepage, and a dashboard that buried the classes they had already bought.

So I restructured the information architecture, simplified every core flow, and built a component system to keep it all consistent. From 2020 to 2021, customers grew from ~8K+ to ~574K+, redemptions from ~5K+ to ~459K+, and certificates from ~4K+ to ~712K+.

My role

I was the lead and only UX designer, managing two design interns and working with PMs, engineers, marketing, and the C-levels. Research, IA, interaction design, prototyping, testing: all of it was mine.

Research

Discovery: Understanding What Was Going Wrong

I ran two research tracks at once: digging through customer service tickets, and getting on the phone with users, both people who bought classes and people who signed up and never came back. The tickets surfaced the top complaints (payment confusion, finding purchased classes, downloading certificates), and the interviews explained why. Most RSK users had low digital literacy, and many had never used an e-learning app before.

I will be honest, I did not have exact per-step drop-off numbers. But the patterns were consistent enough to point at four clear friction points:

1
Registration felt too long.The sign-up form threw too many required fields at people at once. Several interviewees told me they gave up halfway through.
2
Payment was where most people got stuck.The redemption code flow had multiple action buttons with unclear names, and people simply did not know what to do at each step.
3
The dashboard did not show what users were looking for.After buying, people expected to see their class right away. Instead they landed on a banner-filled homepage, and finding a purchased class or certificate took multiple taps through unclear menus.
4
The learning experience gave no sense of progress.Lessons sat in one flat list with no progress indicators or feedback. People could not tell how far along they were or whether they passed.

Building the persona

From these findings I created Fani, a 25-year-old fresh graduate from Bekasi: eager to learn, not too comfortable with digital products, and quick to give up when something feels unclear. She became the filter for every design decision. If Fani would not get it on her first try, it had to be simpler.

Analysis

UX Audit: Mapping the Specific Issues

Before jumping into solutions, I ran a heuristic evaluation using Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules, leaning on "Strive for Consistency" and "Reduce Short-Term Memory Load," the two that matter most for users with low digital literacy. Here is what turned up:

  • Buttons and interactions behaved differently from screen to screen
  • Promotional banners and sliders on the homepage competed with the actual navigation
  • Going from "I want to take a class" to actually enrolling took way too many steps
  • Actions like buying a class gave no clear confirmation, so people were left wondering if it worked

I also looked at Ruangguru (Indonesia's biggest edtech) and Udacity, and two things jumped out that RSK was missing: screens focused on one decision at a time, and progress indicators (bars, badges, grades) that keep people coming back.

Strategy

The Strategic Decision: Structural Redesign Over Quick Fixes

At this point the project could have gone one of two ways.

Option A
Small fixes
Improve button labels, clarify payment instructions, redesign a few cards. Lower risk, faster to ship, but the underlying structure was the problem.
Option B (chosen)
Full structural redesign
Rebuild the information architecture, restructure every core flow, simplify aggressively. More work and risk, but it addressed the root cause.

I pushed for Option B, because the research showed problems everywhere: registration, payment, discovery, learning, certification. The goal became simple. Get every core flow (sign up, find a class, buy it, learn, get the certificate) down to as few steps as possible.

The disagreement about banners

The C-levels and marketing believed more homepage banners meant more sales. I argued the opposite: people arrived with a clear goal (find a class, buy it), the clutter was pushing them away, and the ticket data backed me up.

The evidence won everyone over. We went with a cleaner homepage where promotions no longer compete with the core navigation, and agreed to measure the impact after launch.

Architecture

Information Architecture: Rebuilding the Structure

The old navigation scattered courses, categories, settings, transactions, and support across layers that had grown without a plan. I rebuilt the IA around the three things people actually came to do:

  1. Find classes: Home + Catalog
  2. Learn: Class player + content
  3. Track progress: Dashboard with classes, grades, and certificates

Everything else (settings, transaction history, help center) moved into a side menu, and the bottom tab bar kept only those three goals. I did consider a fourth "Payment" tab but said no: payment is a step in buying a class, not a goal, and it should feel like the natural next step after picking one.

Design

Design Solutions: Connected to Research Findings

Every change here traces straight back to something from the research.

Registration: fewer fields + social sign-in

I made Google sign-in the primary option and cut manual registration down to four fields: name, email, phone, password. Helper text explains upfront that your name must match government records, so nobody finds out the hard way at payment.

Catalog and search: category chips + unified search

I swapped the cluttered homepage grid for a clean catalog: category filter chips, one search that covers both classes and categories, and sort options for rating, newest, and price.

Class details: sticky buy button + clear syllabus

People were not sure what a class actually included before buying. The new detail page keeps the buy button always visible, puts learning outcomes near the top, and adds an expandable syllabus with module names and durations.

Payment: default to Kartu Prakerja + simplified flow

Since almost everyone paid with Kartu Prakerja, I made it the default and replaced the confusing buttons with one voucher-code input plus a short explanation. Other payment methods still exist, just tucked behind a "Change Method" button.

Dashboard: My Class tab

"My Class" became the default dashboard tab. Certificates now live right on the completed class card, and a grade chart shows your scores against the passing threshold at a glance.

Learning experience: playlist structure + quizzes

I turned course content into a playlist-style player with collapsible modules, durations, and clear done/current markers. Quizzes between modules give instant feedback, and a workbook/essay section adds hands-on practice.

Help center: in-app Kartu Prakerja guide

Support was drowning in Kartu Prakerja questions, so I built an in-app help center with step-by-step visual guides for registering and redeeming vouchers. Fewer repeat tickets, and less payment confusion in the first place.

Usability Testing

Testing and Iteration

I ran two rounds of remote usability testing through Maze with first-time digital learners, the exact kind of people Fani represents.

Round 1 results:

Scenario Completion Rate
Create an RSK account 100% (5/5)
Find a specific class ("Menanam Hidroponik") 80% (4/5)
Buy a class using Kartu Prakerja 80% (4/5)
Enter the class and start learning 80% (4/5)

Three issues came up:

  1. People could not tell which classes were Kartu Prakerja-eligible without tapping into each one.
  2. People struggled to find where to buy their redemption code, since nothing pointed them to the external marketplace.
  3. People expected certificates right on the dashboard, without extra digging.

What I changed after Round 1

  • Added a visible "Prakerja" badge on eligible course cards in the catalog.
  • Added Active/Completed filter tabs so finished classes (and their certificate downloads) are one tap away.
  • Added direct marketplace links (Tokopedia, Bukalapak, etc.) on the payment screen so people can buy the code and hop right back.

Round 2 confirmed the fixes worked. Every scenario hit 100% completion.

System

Design System Contribution

Along the way I built the foundational patterns that became RSK's standard going forward:

  • Component library in FigmaButtons, form fields, cards, navigation, and modals, each with all states (default, hover, active, disabled, error).
  • Color and typography tokensPrimary blue, semantic colors for success/error/warning, and a clear type scale.
  • Spacing and layout patterns8px grid, standardized card sizes, predictable padding.
  • Documented interaction patternsBottom-sheet modals, filter chips, tab navigation, and persistent action buttons.

This paid off fast. My two interns could build matching screens without me hovering over every detail, and engineers could pull from the library instead of guessing from mockups.

Team

Mentorship

I set up my two interns' work so they could learn while doing things that mattered:

  • Ownership of specific screens within the design system: real work, not busywork.
  • Regular critique sessions where I walked through my reasoning, including the ideas I rejected.
  • The persona as our shared quality bar: would Fani understand this?

It was my first time managing designers, and it taught me how to scale design quality beyond what I can draw myself.

Reflection

Reflection

What went well

  • "As few steps as possible" kept every decision honest: any new feature or screen had to earn its place.
  • A persona built from real interviews turned opinion debates into one simple question: what would Fani do?
  • The best fixes (Prakerja as the default payment, marketplace links) came out of testing, not my first drafts.

What I would do differently

  • Set up analytics tracking from day oneWe launched without event tracking, so the impact numbers are platform totals rather than per-step funnel data. I would love to have that funnel view.
  • Test with a wider range of users earlierOur participants were mostly urban smartphone users. Testing with rural users on older phones would have caught performance and layout issues sooner.
  • Build a more complete design systemWith more time I would have added usage guidelines, do/don't examples, and accessibility notes for every component.
